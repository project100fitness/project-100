Research & calculation

What the goal asks of the body

The personal record gives the starting numbers. Research explains the physiological demands and the limits of interpreting them. The calculations below use the reported 2 December 2025 baseline, not a new scan.

MUSCLE TASK

+5.35 kg / +13.4%

88.2 lb is about 40.01 kg of estimated skeletal muscle. Reaching 100 lb means 45.36 kg: an additional 11.8 lb.

FAT TASK

22.9% → below 15%

That is a reduction of more than 7.9 percentage points. The required fat loss depends on what happens to muscle and the other fat-free tissues.

LIFE STAGE

46 years / 170 cm

Age and frame shape the comparison. They do not identify an individual hormone level, muscle-loss rate or remaining capacity to grow.

THE REAL CONSTRAINT

Grow while getting leaner

Loading must stimulate adaptation while food and recovery support it. Prolonged energy restriction can weaken the lean-mass response to training. [3]

01 · How much muscle and fat change does the goal imply?

Arithmetic, not a forecast. At 197.1 lb and 22.9% body fat, the reported baseline implies about 45.1 lb fat and 152.0 lb fat-free mass. Skeletal muscle is one part of that fat-free mass; the terms are not interchangeable.

For an illustrative mass-balance model, hold all other fat-free components constant and add 11.8 lb muscle. Fat-free mass becomes about 163.8 lb. At the 15% boundary, that would imply approximately 192.7 lb total weight and 28.9 lb fat: about 16.2 lb less fat and 11.8 lb more muscle than the baseline. A strict below-15% outcome requires going beyond that boundary under the same assumptions.

This model shows why scale weight alone can miss substantial recomposition. It is not a prescribed target weight: hydration, glycogen, bone and other lean components do not necessarily remain constant. The original baseline scan report still needs verification.

02 · What does being 46 actually change?

A whole-body MRI study examined 468 adults aged 18–88. Absolute muscle loss became more apparent toward the end of the forties; height and weight together explained about half the variation in muscle mass. This was a cross-sectional comparison, not a measurement of what any one person loses each year. [1]

Application to this record: age 46 makes midlife context relevant, but does not justify assigning a fixed annual decline, a testosterone value or a personal muscle ceiling. Training history, detraining, symptoms and recovery have to be documented individually. Results in people aged 65 or older cannot be relabelled as a prediction for a 46-year-old.

The 2026 ACSM synthesis covered 137 reviews and more than 30,000 participants. Progressive resistance training improved muscle size and function; higher weekly volume enhanced hypertrophy. That supports the adaptation process, not a guaranteed 5.35 kg gain or a deadline. It also does not certify the existing 15-exercise sessions as the right workload for every body. [2]

03 · Why building muscle while losing fat is harder

Training supplies a growth stimulus; an energy deficit creates a competing constraint. A meta-analysis found smaller lean-mass gains with energy deficiency, and its meta-regression placed the estimated zero-gain point near a 500 kcal/day deficit. This is a study-level estimate, not a personal calorie threshold or evidence that recomposition is impossible. [3]

A controlled four-week trial in young men, with 20 per group, combined intensive exercise and roughly 40% energy restriction. At 2.4 versus 1.2 g protein/kg/day, the higher-protein group gained more lean mass and lost 4.8 ± 1.6 kg fat versus 3.5 ± 1.4 kg. This demonstrates possibility under those conditions; it does not establish expected results, the same muscle gain, or an appropriate regimen at age 46. [5]

04 · Protein figures: what the evidence does and does not say

Across 49 studies and 1,863 participants, a meta-analysis estimated a breakpoint near 1.62 g protein/kg/day for additional fat-free-mass benefit during resistance training; the 95% confidence interval was 1.03–2.20. Using the reported 90.76 kg body weight, 1.62 g/kg works out to about 147 g/day. This is a translation of that research figure, not a newly prescribed intake or a reason to change the recorded recipes. [4]

The study’s mean 0.30 kg additional fat-free mass was the nutrient product-versus-comparator difference, not the total muscle gained from training. Fat-free mass also includes water and other tissue. It cannot be multiplied into a promised rate for the 11.8 lb skeletal-muscle goal.

05 · Why the scan cannot establish a tiny population percentile

The 100 lb goal is a whole-body skeletal-muscle estimate. It is not the same variable as fat-free mass, FFMI or appendicular lean mass. Ranking it against men of similar age and stature requires a matched reference distribution using a compatible measurement method.

In a historical BIA equation validated against MRI in 388 adults, the standard error of estimate was 2.7 kg, about 9%. That illustrates prediction uncertainty; it is not the error bar of this particular InBody unit, nor its day-to-day repeatability. [6]

There is no verified device-matched comparison dataset in this record from which to calculate “top 1%” or “top 0.1%.” The meaningful evidence is the dated change under comparable scan conditions, alongside performance, waist measurements and the photographic record. The next same-unit scan can test progress; it cannot retrospectively verify an unsupported rank.
